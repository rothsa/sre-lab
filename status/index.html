Down or Me

Checks these services every 5 minutes. When something looks wrong, an AI classifier (Jev) decides whether it's a real outage, degraded, planned maintenance, a blip, or a problem on the monitor's side, and how confident it reports being. Verdicts under 80% confidence are marked for a human look.

AI alert triage as a fleet policy

  • Here, one line at 80%. Jev sees the latest result, recent history and how many other services fail, and picks one of five fixed causes with a confidence. TypeSafe, its maker, says it returns typed decisions with calibrated probabilities (early access). An email goes out once per problem, for a real outage or degradation, when Jev is at least 80% sure of the cause and that the owner should hear. A daily cap (JEV_DAILY_CAP, default 200) keeps it optional: failures show without it.
  • Across thousands of alerts, set the line per class. One global threshold is a guess. My advice: set it per cause and service tier, from measured precision, and keep customer-facing pages out of auto-triage until those numbers hold. Meta's root-cause tool withheld low-confidence answers, sacrificing reach in favor of precision.
  • Measure both directions on labeled samples. Precision is the share of alerts that were significant; recall, the share of significant events that alerted. Each week, responders label a random sample of triaged alerts per cause and team, and every suppressed alert is matched against later incidents: each match is a missed page. Check stated confidence against the labeled hit rate; LLMs stating it tend to be overconfident.
  • Who owns its mistakes. The team running the classifier owns its precision and recall like any pager rule, with a target and a review of every missed page. Service owners can opt an alert class out. Other companies' status-page titles are untrusted input; the fixed answer set limits what injected text can do, but it could still nudge a label. A person decides what to do about an outage.
